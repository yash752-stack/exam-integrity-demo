Catching exam malpractice on CCTV, without ever training on cheating

Nobody has labelled examples of cheating in exam-hall footage. So this system learns what is normal for each room, and flags the movement that isn't.

Built by Yash Chaudhary, 2026

One real session

89 minutes, about 40 candidates. Each tick is a moment the maths raised. The six ringed moments were later confirmed as malpractice by a person watching the clips.

How a catch happens

  1. Find every person.

    YOLO11x-pose puts a 17-point skeleton on each candidate, three times a second.

  2. Follow them.

    ByteTrack, tuned for small, faint people in the back rows, keeps one identity per candidate.

  3. Score what is rare.

    For each person, a Fisher score compares 45-second windows of their movement against the rest of the room, and a separate detector catches standing up.

  4. Remove the obvious false alarms.

    Invigilators walking past, people hidden behind others, and tracking glitches are filtered out.

  5. Hand a short list to a person.

    Each alert is a few seconds of video. A reviewer confirms or dismisses it.

Results, including the misses

ResultWhat it means
7 of 14confirmed malpractice moments found, on a frozen benchmark with a room held out from all tuning
~1alert per candidate per hour: a 90-minute room of 40 becomes about 60 short clips
~1 in 3alerts that a reviewer confirms; most of the rest are normal movement, dismissed in seconds
65 of 67seat detections confirmed as real people by a vision-language model (Qwen3-VL-8B); logos and empty desks dropped

What did not work

Running a second pose model on every seat (RTMPose-x) raised the number of people seen per frame from 13 to 24. It found no extra malpractice. Seat crops freeze the very movements worth flagging, so a person standing up stays squashed inside their seat box. Production kept the simpler pipeline.

The misses are mostly small head turns and leaning in the back rows, where people are a few pixels tall.
